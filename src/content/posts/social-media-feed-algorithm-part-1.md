<figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*g3TbWoYOvMKAVZMY_n_WeA.png" /></figure><h3>How to build Algorithm for Social Media Feed — The Basics &amp; System design (Part 1)</h3><p>Around a week ago I was assigned the task “we need a feed like Twitter or linkedin”, I had zero idea how this would be done.</p><p>Feeds look simple from the outside… until you try to make one that’s fast, personalized, doesn’t cost a fortune, and doesn’t die when someone goes viral.</p><p>This is <strong>Part 1</strong> of how we actually built it .</p><h3>Feeds are secretly one of the hardest parts of any social app</h3><p>Everyone wants the same dream:</p><ul><li>Loads in under 1 second (ideally &lt;100 ms)</li><li>Shows posts from people you actually follow</li><li>Throws in some fresh viral content</li><li>Doesn’t show the same 3 people 20 times in a row</li><li>Doesn’t cost $10k/month when you reach 100k users</li></ul><p>Turns out… you can’t get all of that with one naive solution. There are painful trade-offs everywhere.</p><h3>The two classic ways people build feeds (and why both hurt alone)</h3><h3>1. Push everything when someone posts (“fan-out on write”)</h3><p>When Maria posts a cute dog video → we immediately copy that post to the personal “timeline” of <strong>every single follower</strong> she has.</p><p><strong>Pros</strong><br>Reading the feed later is lightning fast — just read your own list!</p><p><strong>Cons</strong><br>If Maria suddenly gets 500,000 followers? You have to write the same post <strong>500,000 times</strong>.<br>Servers scream. Database bills explode. 😭</p><h3>2. Pull everything when the user opens the app (“pull on read”)</h3><p>When you refresh the feed → the system goes:<br>“Okay… find the latest posts from the 312 people Anna follows + some popular stuff right now.”</p><p><strong>Pros</strong><br>Super safe for celebrities. One viral post = one write. No problem.</p><p><strong>Cons</strong><br>If you follow 500+ active people, every refresh becomes a heavy search. Latency kills the experience.</p><h3>What we actually do: a hybrid push + pull system</h3><p>Normal people → push<br>Celebrities → pull</p><p>Simple rule that changed everything.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*BCYio7Gz16W5hskuThrABg.png" /><figcaption>high level architecture diagram</figcaption></figure><h3>Why treat some users as “celebrity” ?</h3><p>Here’s the thing that made my brain click:</p><p>We treat users <strong>completely differently</strong> depending on follower count.</p><pre>&lt;  ~1000 followers   → we push (copy) the post to all followers right now<br>≥ 1000 followers     → we DO NOT copy. We just save it in the author’s own “outbox”</pre><p>We call anyone ≥1000 followers a <strong>“celebrity”</strong> (yes, even if it’s just your friend who posts gym mirror selfies every day 😂)</p><p><strong>Why 1000?</strong><br>It’s the magic number we found after testing.<br>Small enough → pushing is still fast.<br>Big enough → we don’t murder the database the moment someone goes viral.</p><h3>Where everything actually lives (mostly Redis)</h3><p>We lean very heavily on <strong>Redis</strong> because it’s stupidly fast for this kind of workload.</p><p>Here are the most important keys we use every day:</p><ul><li>timeline:anna123 → Anna’s normal feed (only posts from regular-sized accounts)</li><li>celebrity_posts:bob789 → Bob’s own posts (only exists if Bob is “celebrity”)</li><li>post:uuid-abc123-def456 → cached full content of one post (lives ~1 hour)</li><li>celebrities → simple set containing all user IDs with ≥1000 followers</li><li>trending_posts → the current hot/viral posts everyone might see</li></ul><h3>What really happens when someone posts?</h3><h3>Case 1: Normal person posts (&lt;1000 followers)</h3><pre>Maria posts cute cat → we get her followers (usually &lt;300)<br>Then we do: ZADD timeline:john ... post-id<br>           ZADD timeline:sarah ... post-id<br>           ZADD timeline:mike ... post-id<br>           ...</pre><p>Like sending the same postcard to 300 friends. Fast. Cheap. Done.</p><h3>Case 2: Celebrity posts (≥1000 followers)</h3><pre>MrBeast posts → we ONLY do:<br>ZADD celebrity_posts:mrbeast  timestamp  post-id</pre><p>One single write. Extremely cheap.<br>We save the heavy work for later — when people actually want to read his stuff.</p><h3>Quick recap — what we covered in Part 1</h3><ul><li>Normal accounts → <strong>push</strong> posts to followers → reading becomes fast</li><li>Big accounts (“celebrities”) → <strong>pull</strong> on read → doesn’t explode on viral posts</li><li>Almost everything important lives in <strong>Redis sorted sets</strong> (perfect for “latest first” timelines)</li><li>We automatically trim old posts (keep ~500 latest per user)</li></ul><h3>Some References</h3><ul><li><strong>Twitter’s Timeline Architecture-</strong><a href="https://blog.twitter.com/engineering/en_us/topics/infrastructure/2017/the-infrastructure-behind-twitter-scale">https://blog.twitter.com/engineering/en_us/topics/infrastructure/2017/the-infrastructure-behind-twitter-scale</a></li><li><a href="https://www.infoq.com/presentations/Twitter-Timeline-Scalability/">https://www.infoq.com/presentations/Twitter-Timeline-Scalability/</a></li><li><strong>Designing a News Feed (System Design)-</strong><a href="https://www.educative.io/courses/grokking-the-system-design-interview">https://www.educative.io/courses/grokking-the-system-design-interview</a></li></ul><p>In <strong>Part 2</strong> we’ll get to the really fun part: How we actually decide <strong>what shows up first</strong>, how we mix friends + viral posts, how we avoid boring repetitive feeds, and how guest users get a feed without even logging in.</p><p>If you enjoyed this and excited for Part 2, feel free to follow me here on Medium — or come say hi on X/Twitter <a href="http://x.com/manujdxt"><strong>@manujdxt</strong></a> 😄 I usually post small dev tips, architecture breakdowns, and the occasional confused dev moment.</p><p>Thanks for reading — see you in Part 2 next week! 🚀</p>
